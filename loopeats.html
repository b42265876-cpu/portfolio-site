The circular economy challenge

From a circular brief to household food waste

Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs

How might we use design thinking to deliver a more circular economy in and for the city of Bristol?

Why household food waste?

The brief asked us to identify a local opportunity for circular design and develop a testable response in Bristol.

Four opportunity areas were compared through secondary research and a team rating exercise. Scores reflect our team’s comparative ratings.

Food waste received the highest overall score in our comparison, leading us to explore it further through primary research.

Our research focusHow does edible food become surplus, and what prevents people from using or sharing it before disposal?

Primary research

We explored food routines, the gap between intention and action, and barriers to surplus-food sharing.

What this suggests

The findings suggest that willingness to reduce waste does not always translate into action. Everyday effort and concerns about sharing also need to be addressed.

Understanding the barriers behind food waste

We brought the research findings together to understand what made reducing waste difficult in everyday life.

Initial hypothesis

Greater awareness and better food management could help reduce waste.

Revised understanding

Willingness to reduce waste did not always translate into action. Practical and social barriers also shaped participation.

Everyday effort

Limited time made food management difficult to sustain. Reducing waste had to compete with other daily priorities.

Planning difficulties

Meal planning difficulties and promotions contributed to overbuying, leaving surplus food without a clear plan for use.

Social discomfort

Giving and receiving surplus food could feel awkward. Perceived charity could make collecting food uncomfortable.

Trust and privacy

Uncertainty about food safety and concerns about privacy could discourage participation in food sharing.

Core insightWillingness was only a starting point. Acting on it depended on everyday effort, social comfort and trust.

From insights to product strategy

Research findings shaped a service concept that combines an app with a shared kitchen to support convenient, trustworthy and discreet food exchange.

HMW question

How might we make sharing and collecting surplus food convenient and trustworthy, while protecting privacy and dignity?

Service concept

An app supports food listings, discovery and collection. A shared kitchen makes it possible to leave, store and collect food independently.

Design principles and responses

01 · Sharing requires time and coordination.

Reduce everyday effort

Simple food listings and clear collection instructions.

02 · Direct exchanges can feel uncomfortable.

Protect privacy and dignity

Discreet participation and collection without meeting the provider.

03 · Food safety uncertainty discourages collection.

Make trust visible

Visible food details, storage guidance and collection deadlines.

User feedback and design iteration

What users valued

Users valued the sense of contribution and community connection, and expressed willingness to share surplus food.

Where users struggled

Conflicting entry points.
Unclear distinction between Fridge and Shared Kitchen.
Limited categories and content previews.
Insufficient feedback after actions.

Design priorities

Clarify navigation and feature labels.
Improve categories and listing previews.
Make drop-off and collection status visible.

Final design — Today

Quick action · Food discover · Profile

Add, Share and Find stay on Today to reduce steps to the most frequent waste-prevention actions.

Use what you have prioritises existing food; Near you connects local surplus.
